Why companies audit themselves

At Cedar Valley, the calibration procedure says every gauge is checked by an accredited lab once a year. Nobody has looked at how that works on the shop floor since the procedure was written. A new line was added, two people changed jobs, and the list of due dates lives in a spreadsheet on one person's laptop. Is the procedure still followed? Nobody knows until someone looks.

A system that nobody checks drifts

Every company writes down how it works: procedures, work instructions, checklists. Then real life happens. People find shortcuts. A new product does not fit the old checklist. The person who owned a task moves to another job and nobody takes it over.

None of this is anyone's bad intent. It is what happens to every set of rules over time. An internal audit is how a company finds out how far it has drifted, before a customer or an outside auditor finds out for it.

What an internal audit is

An internal audit is a planned check that the company runs on itself, with its own trained staff. It asks two questions:

  1. Do we do what our rules say we do?
  2. Do those rules work?

The auditors compare what they see with what should be. They read records, watch the work and talk to the people doing it. Everything they report must rest on facts they can show, such as a record, a photo or a date on a label.

An audit looks for gaps in the system, not for people to blame. If a gauge is overdue, the question is why the system let that happen, not who forgot.

Who asks for it

Customers expect it, and many demand it in their contracts. The standards a company is certified to require it:

  • ISO 9001, the general quality standard, requires internal audits at planned intervals.
  • ISO 13485 for medical devices and AS9100 for aerospace require them too, with extra rules.

Once a year, an outside certification body audits the company to renew its certificate. One of the first things it checks is whether the company runs its own audits and acts on what they find.

Who does what

Quality manager

Plans which audits run this year and tracks what they find.

Auditors

Trained staff from other departments. They never audit their own work.

Auditees

The people whose work is audited. They show how the work is done.

Management

Gives the time and people, and reads the results.

Cedar Valley borrows its auditors from other departments. Sam is a process engineer and Lina is a buyer. Neither works in machining, so neither checks their own work.

What comes out of it

An audit produces findings: the places where what the auditors saw differs from what should be. Some findings are serious and need a lasting fix. Some are small slips. Some are only a warning sign. The next chapters follow all three kinds.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the audit as a test that people pass or fail. People then hide problems, and the audit finds nothing useful.
  • Letting people audit their own area. They see what they expect to see.
  • Auditing only before the certification visit. A yearly rush finds less than a steady plan.

For ISO readers

ISO 9001 clause 9.2 requires internal audits at planned intervals, to show that the quality system meets the company's own requirements and the standard, and that it works. The auditors must be objective and impartial. ISO 19011 gives guidance on how to run audits and audit programs. This chapter covers the purpose of clause 9.2.
